Australia's Baby Drought

Why Australians are having fewer children — and what it means for the country.

Cong Duy Dang — 36234567  ·  29 May 2026

Part 1

Total Fertility Rate

Australia's fertility rate reached a record low in 2024, sitting well below the 2.1 births per woman needed for population replacement.

The ideal replacement level 2.1 births per woman
Australia’s fertility rate 1.48 children per woman in 2024 Lowest since records began

National fertility rate over time, 1927-2024

Line tracks children per woman; the dashed rule marks replacement level.

Australia's total fertility rate has fallen to a record low of 1.48 children per woman in 2024 — less than half the Baby Boom peak of 3.55 reached in 1961. The long decline began almost immediately after the contraceptive pill became widely available in the early 1960s, with the rate dropping below the replacement level of 2.1 for the first time in 1976. A brief recovery followed the introduction of the Federal Government's Baby Bonus in 2004, which provided a one-off payment of up to $5,000 per birth — but the effect proved temporary. The scheme was phased out on 1 March 2014 and replaced by the Newborn Supplement, a more targeted payment tied to the parental leave system. Since then, the fertility rate has fallen sharply and without interruption, reaching its lowest point on record in 2024.

State by State

The decline is national, but its pace differs sharply between states and territories.

Every state and territory recorded a lower fertility rate in 2024 than in 2014. The ACT experienced the steepest fall, dropping from 1.738 to 1.273 children per woman, a decline of 26.8%. Western Australia, Tasmania, Queensland and South Australia also fell by more than one-fifth over the decade. The Northern Territory declined the least, from 2.041 to 1.629, and remains the highest-fertility jurisdiction in 2024. The dashed lines mark the national average fertility rates in 2014 and 2024. By the end of the decade, most states had converged close to that level, showing that fertility rates are not only falling, but becoming more similar across much of the country.

Part 3

Where Babies Are Born

Fertility rates vary sharply at the local level — from inner-city lows to remote community highs — far beyond what state averages reveal.

LGA fertility rates, 2024

This map shows two local stories at once: colour shows the fertility rate, while each spike shows how many babies were registered in that LGA in 2024.

Fertility rate and birth count by LGA, 2024

Area colour maps children per woman; spike height maps births registered.

Each vertical spike marks an LGA and grows taller as births registered in 2024 increase. Hover any local area or spike for its LGA, state, births, and TFR. Unpublished values appear as N/P.

Australia's fertility landscape tells a clear geographic story, but the spikes add the missing punchline: the places with the lowest fertility rates can still be places where many babies are born. Melbourne's CBD recorded just 0.54 children per woman in 2024 — the lowest of any LGA in the country — followed closely by Adelaide (0.57), Perth (0.61) and Sydney (0.73). These inner-city councils are not empty of births; they are places where births are spread across a much larger population of adults, young professionals, students and renters.

At the other extreme, the highest fertility rates are found in small, remote Indigenous communities. Cherbourg in Queensland recorded 3.99 — the highest in Australia — followed by Hope Vale (3.95) and Doomadgee (3.40), all in Queensland. These communities have distinct cultural, social and economic circumstances that shape very different family patterns. But because their populations are small, a high fertility rate does not always produce a tall birth spike.

That is why the map needs both layers. Colour answers "how high is fertility here?", while spike height answers "how many babies are actually being born here?". Brisbane has a low TFR of 1.19, yet it has the tallest spike with 12,514 births. Gold Coast (6,566), Casey (6,152), Wyndham (5,828), Blacktown (5,651) and Logan (5,590) also stand out as major birth-volume LGAs. The big baby counts are therefore concentrated in populous urban and suburban councils, even when their fertility rates are only average or below average.

In the vast interior of Australia — the outback stretching across the centre of the continent — fertility rates tend to sit in the moderate-to-high range, typically between 2.0 and 3.0. This band runs through central Queensland, outback New South Wales, South Australia's interior and the Northern Territory. Much of this reflects a combination of younger populations, stronger cultural ties to family, and economic conditions that differ markedly from coastal cities. Notably, many of these central LGAs appear unfilled on the map — not because fertility is zero, but because the population is so small that the ABS does not publish a reliable rate.

Birth volume by state, 2024

The mini pie map tells a different story from the LGA fertility map. Large states dominate the number of babies born because they have more people, while the map beneath still compares fertility rates.

Birth share and fertility rate by state, 2024

Mini pies show each state’s share of national births; map colour shows TFR.

Each mini pie shows that state or territory's share of national births registered in 2024. Colour still shows the total fertility rate, making the gap between birth volume and fertility rate easier to compare.

Birth volume is concentrated in Australia's most populous states. New South Wales recorded the most births in 2024, with 89,030 babies, followed by Victoria (81,193) and Queensland (58,838). Western Australia sits well behind the three eastern states at 30,541 births, while South Australia recorded 18,444. The smallest totals are in the smaller jurisdictions: Tasmania (5,509), the ACT (5,218) and the Northern Territory (3,521). This shows that the number of babies born is driven mainly by population size rather than fertility rate. The Northern Territory has the highest TFR, but the fewest births; New South Wales has the largest birth count even though its fertility rate is not the highest.

Part 4

Motherhood is moving later

Comparing 1976, 2000 and 2024 shows the age profile shifting right: fertility once peaked in the late twenties, crossed over around 2000, and now peaks among women aged 30-34.

Age-specific fertility profile, 1976-2024

Ridges show births per 1,000 women by age group across three benchmark years.

The age profile of motherhood has shifted sharply to the right. In 1976, fertility was concentrated in the twenties, with the highest age-specific fertility rate among women aged 25-29 at 146.2 births per 1,000 women, followed closely by women aged 20-24. By 2000, the peak had moved to 30-34, narrowly overtaking 25-29. In 2024, that later pattern is much clearer: women aged 30-34 now record the highest rate at 106.0, while fertility among women aged 20-24 has fallen to just 30.6. At the same time, births among women aged 35-39 and 40-44 have become more common than they were in the 1970s. The overall trend is not just fewer births, but later births.

Age-specific fertility by state, 2024

Age-specific fertility shows where births concentrate in the life course — and how that pattern differs between states.

Age-specific fertility peaks by state, 2024

The state heatmap shows that the delayed motherhood pattern is now national. In every state and territory, the highest age-specific fertility rate is among women aged 30-34. New South Wales, South Australia and Victoria have the strongest peaks in this age band, all sitting around 111 births per 1,000 women. The ACT has the lowest fertility among younger women, especially at ages 15-19 and 20-24, but its 35-39 rate remains close to the national pattern, showing how strongly births are concentrated later in life. The Northern Territory stands out in the opposite direction: it still peaks at 30-34, but has much higher fertility among teenagers and women in their early twenties than any other jurisdiction. Overall, the heatmap confirms that Australia's fertility decline is not evenly spread across age groups; births have shifted away from the teens and twenties and are now concentrated in the early thirties.

Part 5

The Forces Behind It

This is where the story turns from what is happening to why. The global view comes first, showing how low Australian fertility sits compared with much of the world — before we turn to two pressures closer to home.

The global comparison shows that low fertility is increasingly common across both developed and developing economies. Most high-income countries sit well below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman, including Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, Japan and South Korea. But the same pattern is also visible in parts of developing Asia, where China, Thailand and Singapore are already very low and India is just below replacement. The major exceptions are concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, where countries such as Chad, Somalia, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Niger remain above five children per woman, as well as Afghanistan and Yemen. This divide may reflect differences in income, urban housing pressure, education, access to family planning, child-care costs, gender expectations and the age structure of each population.

Education and fertility

This scatterplot compares tertiary education enrolment with total fertility rate across countries, with circle size showing each country's 2024 population.

Education enrolment versus fertility rate

Each point is a country; x maps tertiary enrolment, y maps fertility, size maps population.

The education scatterplot tests one part of that explanation. Across the joined country data, tertiary education enrolment and fertility have a moderate negative relationship: countries with higher education enrolment tend to sit lower on the fertility scale. Australia sits inside this low-fertility, high-education cluster rather than outside it. South Korea is an extreme version of the same pattern, with very high tertiary enrolment and exceptionally low fertility. China also sits low on fertility despite its much larger population, while India remains higher than Australia but is moving closer to the low-fertility group. The pattern should be read as an association rather than proof of direct causation: education often moves together with urbanisation, later marriage, higher female labour-force participation, access to contraception and the rising opportunity cost of having children.

The housing trap

This chart compares Australia's fertility rate with the real home price index from 2009 to 2024.

Home prices and fertility in Australia, 2009-2024

Bars map the real home price index; the line maps total fertility rate.

The housing chart points to one possible pressure behind Australia's decline. Between 2009 and 2024, the real home price index rose from 92.13 to 133.5, an increase of about 45%, while the total fertility rate fell from 1.97 to 1.481, a drop of about 25%. The relationship is not perfectly mechanical: fertility briefly rose in 2021 even as prices surged, and house prices eased in 2023 while fertility still fell. But the broad direction is clear. Over the period when housing became more expensive, fertility moved steadily lower, suggesting that affordability may be one of several forces making family formation harder, alongside delayed partnering, insecure work, child-care costs and changing expectations around parenthood.

Part 6

The Ageing Future

The baby drought does not end with fewer prams today. Carried forward, the same decline reshapes the entire population — a narrower base of young Australians, and a far heavier top of older ones.

Population pyramid: 2024 to 2071 projection

Australia’s medium projection shows the base narrowing while the older population expands year by year.

Population age structure, 2024-2071

Animated pyramid uses ABS medium projection (Series B) by sex and five-year age group.

The animated pyramid makes the long-term consequence of low fertility visible. In 2024, Australia’s age structure is still relatively balanced: children aged 0-14 make up 17.9% of the population, close to the 17.6% aged 65 and over. By 2071, the population is larger overall, so the number of children also rises slightly. The important change is the balance of the structure: children aged 0-14 fall from 17.9% to 14.6% of the population, while the 65+ share rises to 25.1%. The oldest group changes most dramatically in absolute terms, with Australians aged 85 and over growing from about 0.59 million to 2.05 million. That shift means young people become a smaller part of the population supporting a much larger older cohort, increasing pressure on health care, pensions, aged care and the working-age tax base.

When the age groups change rank

The pyramid shows the structure year by year. This sorted stream shows the same story as a race: each ribbon is an age band, and when one band overtakes another, their ribbons cross.

Age bands re-sorted by share, 2024-2071

Ribbon thickness maps each broad age band's share of the population; ribbons are re-ranked at each plotted year so crossings mark overtakes.

Normalising every year to 100% strips out raw population growth and shows the changing mix. In 2024 the 65+ band is the smallest of the five, at about 17.6% of the population, just below children aged 0-14 (17.9%), while the 45-64 band is the largest at 23.8%. Within a decade the order has been rewritten: by 2034 the 65+ ribbon has climbed past the 0-14, 15-29 and 30-44 bands to become the second largest share. By 2071 it has also overtaken 45-64, becoming the largest band at about 25.1%. The most striking movement is the 0-14 band sliding to the bottom, reaching about 14.6% by 2071 as the 65+ band rises over it. Each crossing point is a moment when Australia's population mix has, in effect, swapped a younger cohort for an older one.

Part 7

The Replacement Gap

Australia does not need a small correction. It needs a structural reversal.

Australia’s population futures, 2024-2071

Three ABS projections — low, medium and high — show how big Australia’s total population could grow by 2071.

This final chart asks the simplest question of all: how big will Australia get? Each line is one of the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ official projections for the country’s total population — the total number of people living in Australia — from 2024 out to 2071. The three lines differ only in their assumptions about the future: the high path assumes more births and migration, the low path assumes fewer, and the bold medium path sits between them. The shaded band between the lines is the range of futures Australia could plausibly end up in.

The lines start almost together at about 27 million in 2024, then fan apart. By 2071 the gap between the highest and lowest paths has opened to more than 11 million people — roughly the combined population of Sydney and Melbourne today — and how high Australia lands depends heavily on births. To keep a population steady without relying on migration, each generation needs about 2.1 children per woman; Australia sits at just 1.48, a shortfall of 0.62. That shortfall is the replacement gap, and its real cost is not only a smaller population but an older one: in every path there are fewer children at the bottom of the pyramid and many more Australians over 65, leaving a shrinking share of working-age adults to support them. Closing that gap would take far more than a nudge — it would take the structural reversal this story began with.
